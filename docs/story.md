# The Shallow Water Story

The single source of truth for the Shallow Water series narrative. Every game tells one chapter of this story; no game invents story outside its chapter.

## Premise

Varana is a modern island nation that never stopped moving by water. Its canals — cut in the industrial age, modelled on the canals of England — still thread every town, and an entire underclass lives afloat on them. The people of the water are called **bandits**: a slur coined by the press, worn now with pride. Above them sits **the Charter**, the private consortium that bought Varana's waterways from a broke government and has been squeezing the people who live on them ever since — licence fees invented overnight, moorings seized, boats impounded, communities drained.

The player is **Wren**, a drifter with a murky past who came to the canals to disappear. The water folk took him in, no questions asked. When the Charter starts tearing that community apart, Wren stops drifting. The series follows his rise from lone narrowboater to the man who takes the Charter down — one steward, one district, one family at a time.

## The World

**The Cut** is what boaters call the canal network, and it is the spine of the game world: locks, tunnels, aqueducts, flooded basins, junction villages, boatyard towns, and the slow brown miles between them. Life on the Cut runs at four miles an hour in a country that has forgotten how to slow down — and that contrast is the game's texture. Gritty things happen here, but the Cut has a dark, dry sense of humour about all of it.

**The Charter** presents itself as a heritage trust — glossy adverts, restored towpaths, artisan marinas for weekenders. Underneath it is a racket: enforcement stewards with quotas, debt manufactured through impossible licence rules, seized boats resold through shell companies, and, at the top, a handful of old Varanese families who treat the nation's water as a private estate.

**The bandits** are not one gang. They are boat-dwelling families, cash-in-hand traders, smugglers, poachers, breakdown mechanics, and pirate radio operators — a loose society with its own code: the water belongs to everyone, you stop for a stranded boat, and you never talk to the Charter.

## Characters

**Wren** — the player. Forties, quiet, capable in ways he doesn't explain. He lives aboard *Last Orders*, a tired 57-foot narrowboat he is forever patching up. Where he learned to fight, pick locks, and run an engine dry of questions is deliberately untold — his past is the series' held-back card, revealed across later games. What the demo establishes: he owes the water folk a debt of belonging, and he pays his debts.

**Maud Halloran** — keeper of the boatyard at Tapley Junction and unofficial mayor of its floating community. Sharp-tongued, unimpressed by everyone, the first person who treated Wren as one of their own. She hands out work, warnings, and the demo's moral centre.

**Edwin Voss** — the Charter's District Steward for the Tapley cut. A small man given a uniform and a quota. Voss is the demo's antagonist: petty, cruel within the rules he writes himself, and — as the demo's final beat reveals — merely the lowest rung of a much taller ladder.

## The Series Arc

The series is Wren versus the Charter, told bottom-up: each game defeats one layer and exposes the one above it.

### Game 1 — Shallow Water Demo: "Tapley Junction"

The whole demo lives on one stretch of the Cut around Tapley Junction. Voss's stewards impound the boat of Maud's oldest neighbour over a licence debt nobody can trace, with the boatyard itself next on the seizure list. Wren works the junction — odd jobs, favours, small sabotage — until the community trusts him with the real job: get the impounded boats back and get rid of Voss. The demo ends with Voss ruined by his own paperwork, the boats cut loose and home, and one loose thread pulled: the seizure orders were never Voss's idea. They came from above, signed only with a wax stamp — the mark of the Charter proper. Chapter closed; series opened.

### Later Games (sketch)

Game 2 takes Wren up the ladder into a canal city and the Charter's regional machine. Game 3 reaches the capital, the founding families, and the truth about why Wren came to the water in the first place. These are sketches, not commitments — each gets defined fully in its own chapter before it is built.

## Themes

Who owns the commons — water as something lived on versus something owned. Belonging versus drifting — Wren's arc is learning that disappearing and belonging can't both be true. And slowness as defiance — four miles an hour as a way of refusing the world the Charter is selling. Every mechanic should serve at least one of these; a mechanic that contradicts them is wrong.
