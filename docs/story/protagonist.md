# Granger

**Arthur Granger.** Forty-five in 1938. Known on the cut as Granger, at the barracks as Sergeant, in the Tame Otter as the bloke off the boat. Nobody uses Arthur.

## What is true

- Born 1893 in the Potteries. Enlisted 1914, Staffordshire Regiment. A rifleman, then a marksman, then by 1918, at twenty-five, the best shot in the division and a decorated one: a Distinguished Conduct Medal for a day on the Sambre he has never described. The word sniper was not used in front of him. Stayed on after the war as a musketry instructor and put ten thousand recruits and cadets through the range in Hopwas Hays Wood. Discharged 1932 on a pension that would not keep a dog.
- Twenty years after the war he is fitter than the cadets. He runs the wood before the flags go up, swims the Tame in October, and can lie still on a wet bank for six hours. He does not drink on the boat and does not smoke on a job. The body is the tool and he keeps it like the rifle.
- He is a gun for hire. His trade is the vantage point: a mill roof, a bridge parapet, a wooded bank, a church tower, found on foot the day before, left by water the night after. He takes people out for money and he takes bounty, recovery and watching work when the money is thinner. He does not ask why. He asks where, when, and how the client wants it to look.
- He moves about the country unseen. The cut is his road: two thousand miles of water nobody watches, through the back of every town in England, with a towpath for walking and a boat that draws no attention because it is old. He can be in Birmingham by Tuesday and nobody at Hopwas will have seen him leave.
- Being forgettable is survival. Nothing flashy: no livery, no brass, no dog, no story in the pub. He dresses like every other man on the towpath and is described, when he is described at all, as ordinary. A man who is remembered is a man who is found.
- He always moors outside the villages, never in them: a quarter mile beyond the last house, under trees, where the towpath has no reason to go. Nobody sees the boat for long and nobody sees the gun at all.
- The gun is hidden and it is in pieces. The scoped rifle comes apart into the stock, the barrel and the scope, and lives that way in a place on the boat no search would find. He does not carry it. In the game he walks like any other man, hands empty, and only when the story needs the shot does he take the pieces out and put them together, which takes him under a minute and is a scene every time.
- He keeps moving. A night here, two there, rarely the same mooring twice in a month and rarely a word to anyone. Most of his life is the towpath, not the job. Hopwas is the one place he comes back to, because it is the best cover in England for a man like him: he knows the wood better than the range staff, the flags give him a reason to carry a rifle, the gunfire gives him a reason for the sound, and the village has known his boat for six years without ever quite knowing him.
- The boat is *Sparrow*. A 1912 Josher motor with a single-cylinder Bolinder that has to be started with a blowlamp and reversed by stopping it and starting it backwards. He did not name her and has never painted the name out.
- He owns three guns. A service rifle he should not have. A sporting rifle with a scope that he bought legally and that every constable in the county has an opinion about. A pistol he does not talk about. All three live in a locker the size of a cupboard.

## Who pays him

The people with money and a motive, and in 1938 that is every side at once. The British Union wants a Jewish trader off a Birmingham wharf. A Communist Party backer wants a strikebreaker to stop breaking strikes. A mill board wants a union man gone before the vote. A haulage firm wants a boatman who saw too much. A man at the Ministry wants a file, and the man carrying it. Granger has worked for all of them and none of them know about the others, and none of them would admit to knowing him.

He has one rule and it is not a moral one: he does not take a job in the seven miles. Home stays clean. The demo is the first time he breaks it.

## What he wants

In 1938, to keep moving and be left alone. He would tell you he takes the work for the money. He takes it because on a roof at four hundred yards he is the best in England at something, and in the village he is a man with a long gun and no wife.

What he comes to want, one chapter at a time, is for the country to work. He has seen a cadet, a boat family and a picket line look after each other better than any company or Ministry ever looked after them. He has also seen who stops that from spreading. He does not want power for itself. He wants it because nobody else who could get it would use it properly, and he is the only person he trusts to do the bad things it takes.

## What he will not say

He does not volunteer war stories. He does not answer questions about the war or the medal. When he is drunk he gets quieter, not louder. The series honours this: one true thing about the war per chapter, never told by him, always found. See the rule in [series.md](series.md).

The writers know something the player does not. In the last month of the war he was ordered to shoot a man on his own side, and he did it. The medal came a week later, and the officer who gave the order is alive and on the rise. This is the deepest water in the series. It is told in Game 4, and in Game 6 he says it himself, in public, to bring the Officer down. It is the one time in the series he volunteers anything.

## How he reads

- Speaks little. Complete sentences when he does. No slang he did not bring back from the army.
- Watches hands, exits, rooflines and the water level.
- Polite to boat families, women, and anyone in a uniform lower than sergeant. Short with clerks. Silent with officers. Courteous to clients and never seen with one twice.
- Drinks in whatever pub is by the water that night, one seat, back to the wall. At Hopwas it is the Tame Otter.
- Cabin: oil, wet wool, gun solvent, a wireless he keeps tuned to the dance bands and turns off when the news comes on. Roses and castles under the soot on the cabin doors, painted by a woman he never met for a family that sold up.

## What the player can and cannot make him do

The player chooses the jobs, the client, the vantage point, the route in and the route out, and the mercy. Later in the series the player also chooses the allies, the sacrifices and who pays for each rise. The player cannot make him take company livery, serve another man's party, take a bribe to stop, or explain himself. He does the bad things the climb demands, and the player chooses how and to whom, never whether. When a choice would make him a different man, the game does not offer it.
