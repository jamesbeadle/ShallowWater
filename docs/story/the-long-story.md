# The long story

The spine of the series is a question with an answer that keeps moving: **who is bleeding the cut, and where is it going?** Every chapter answers it for one stretch of water and one year, and the answer is always somebody higher up the bank.

## The Pound (demo, autumn 1936)

A deserter runs from Whittington with a reason. Askew is paid to bring him back and finds the reason instead: the depot armoury is short, someone has been signing for rifles that are not there, and the rifles are leaving under coal on company boats that only run at night. The strike that shut the junctions was not a wage dispute but a curtain: the company let the keepers walk, dropped the planks, and turned seven miles of water into a private yard where crates could move from boat to lorry at Peel's Wharf with no police on the water and no boats passing. The strike fund is Dunmore's money through a union man who did not ask.

The demo ends with Askew knowing this and holding one crate, and choosing: return the boy, run the boy, or bury the boy's story with him. Then the strike breaks, or he breaks the junction. Either way the water opens. What he knows goes with him into the city.

*One true thing about the war:* Garner, drunk, says Askew was the best shot the depot ever had and that it was not the shooting they gave him the stripe for.

## The Cut (Game 1, 1936 to 1937)

The junctions open. Askew follows the crates: Fazeley to the Birmingham and Fazeley, up the Curdworth locks into the city's black water, the BCN, where a hundred miles of loops and basins hide anything. The guns are going south on the new Grand Union motors, the ones the company is so proud of, to Limehouse and a ship for Spain. Dunmore is the road end. The water end is a Grand Union agent at Braunston who thinks he is a patriot. Tench has moved to a bigger toll house and kept the second set of books.

The player runs the Grand Union from Birmingham to London: Hatton, Braunston, Blisworth, the Tring summit, Paddington, Regent's, Limehouse. Every stretch has its own crime and its own client; the crates are the reason to keep going. It ends on the tideway at Limehouse with the ship sailing and Askew able to stop a man but not a trade, and a name he did not want: the Officer's, on a letter Tench should not have kept.

The abdication is on every wireless in December. Nobody on the cut cares.

*One true thing:* The pistol in the locker is a German officer's. Askew did not take it from a German.

## The Flight (Game 2, 1938)

North. Fradley, the Trent and Mersey, the Potteries he was born in, Harecastle tunnel, the Cheshire locks. Ada Skerritt dies and the funeral is the cut's last great turnout. The road firm that bought Dunmore's lorries is buying boats now, to scrap them. The bleeding is not guns any more but the boats themselves: sold cheap, sunk for insurance, broken up for the iron that a coming war will want. Lil Skerritt runs *Patience* with a motor she cannot afford. Askew finds the men sinking boats and finds who is paying, and it is a Ministry list, and the list has the Officer's initials.

*One true thing:* Lil finds a photograph on the boat. Askew in 1918, with a man whose face is scratched out.

## The Blackout (Game 3, 1940 to 1944)

The war Askew came home from comes to the water. Blackout on the cut, women crewing the boats, the barracks full, Garner's boys going to France and coming back or not. Bounty work becomes deserters again, and black market, and a saboteur who is not one. The Officer is a colonel and Askew is asked, in the sergeants' mess he has not entered in ten years, to do the thing he did in 1918 once more, for the same reason. This is the chapter that says what he did. He says no, and the price of no is the whole chapter.

*The true thing:* the whole of it. After this, the series cannot go back, and Askew is different.

## The Tideway (Game 4, 1948)

Nationalisation. The cut belongs to the state and the state belongs to men like the Officer, now sitting for a Midlands seat. Tench is at the Ministry. Askew is fifty-three and the Bolinder is dying. The bleeding is now official: a report that will decide which canals live and which are let go, and the report is being written by the people who own the lorries. The last chapter of the first arc is Askew on the Thames, out of the cut for the first time, in water with a tide.

## After the tideway

The series does not end. Each later chapter takes a piece of the map and a year in the long decline: the closures of the fifties, the last coal contracts, the first hire boats laughing at the working men. Askew gets older. Lil gets the boat. One chapter is called *The Last Boat* and it is the one where he dies, and the writers know which year and have not told anyone.

## The spine, stated once

The cut is being drained on purpose. Not by one villain but by a class of men who own the road and sit on the boards and write the reports, and who used the water while it was useful and are letting it silt now that it is not. Askew cannot stop it. Nobody could. The series is about a man who keeps his boat afloat in water that is being let out from under him, and takes what work he can find, and does it well, and keeps his mouth shut about the one thing he could say that would sink the man at the top. Whether he ever says it is the series' last question.
