# The long story

The spine of the series is a question with an answer that keeps moving: **who is bleeding the cut, and where is it going?** Every chapter answers it for one stretch of water and one year, and the answer is always somebody higher up the bank.

## Locked (demo, autumn 1938)

A cadet runs from Whittington with a reason. Askew is paid to bring him back and finds the reason instead: the depot armoury is short, someone has been signing for rifles that are not there, and the rifles are leaving under coal on company boats that only run at night. The strike that shut the junctions was not a wage dispute but a curtain: the company let the keepers walk, dropped the planks, and turned seven miles of water into a private yard where crates could move from boat to lorry at Peel's Wharf with no police on the water and no boats passing. The strike fund is Dunmore's money through a union man who did not ask.

The demo also breaks his one rule: three clients, three sides, each wanting a man dead inside the seven miles he keeps clean. It ends with Askew knowing all this and holding one crate, and choosing: return the cadet, run the cadet, or bury the cadet's story with him. Then the strike breaks, or he breaks the junction. Either way the water opens. What he knows goes with him, and so does the need to be somewhere else.

*One true thing about the war:* Garner, drunk, says Askew was the best shot the depot ever had and that it was not the shooting they gave him the medal for.

## The Grand Union (Game 2, 1938 to 1939)

The junctions open. Askew follows the crates: Fazeley to the Birmingham and Fazeley, up the Curdworth locks into the city's black water, the BCN, where a hundred miles of loops and basins hide anything. The guns are going south on the new Grand Union motors, the ones the company is so proud of, towards a ship for Spain. Dunmore is the road end. The water end is a Grand Union agent at Braunston who thinks he is a patriot. Tench has moved to a bigger toll house and kept the second set of books.

The player runs the Grand Union from Birmingham to the edge of London: Hatton, Braunston, Blisworth, the Tring summit, Bulls Bridge, Paddington. Every stretch has its own crime and its own client; the crates are the reason to keep going. It ends at Paddington with the guns gone down onto the tideway where a canal boat has no business, and a name he did not want: the Officer's, on a letter Tench should not have kept.

Munich is on every wireless in September and the trenches are going in at Birmingham. Nobody on the cut cares.

*One true thing:* The pistol in the locker is a German officer's. Askew did not take it from a German.

## The Tidal Thames (Game 3, 1939)

A canal boat on a tidal river. *Sparrow* was never built for it: the tide runs faster than the engine, the wash of a tug can sink her, and there is nowhere to moor out of sight. Limehouse, the Regent's Canal, the docks, the river down to the estuary and the forts. The ship is here, and so is everyone who wants it stopped or sailed. Askew learns a water that does not forgive, among people who do not forget, and for the first time cannot simply leave.

War is declared on a Sunday in September, halfway through. The docks fill with the wrong kind of attention. It ends with the ship sailed or sunk and Askew going back up the cut, sixteen days to Hopwas, with a war behind him and one ahead.

*One true thing:* Lil Skerritt finds a photograph on the boat. Askew in 1918, with a man whose face is scratched out.

## Shallow Water 4 and after (working titles)

The chapters after the Thames are numbered until their water is known. The writers hold this much:

- **The war on the water.** Blackout on the cut, women crewing the boats, the barracks full, Garner's cadets going to France and coming back or not. Deserters again, black market, a saboteur who is not one. The Officer is a colonel, and Askew is asked, in the sergeants' mess he has not entered in ten years, to do the thing he did in 1918 once more, for the same reason. This is the chapter that says what he did. He says no, and the price of no is the whole chapter. After it the series cannot go back.
- **The north.** Fradley, the Trent and Mersey, the Potteries he was born in, Harecastle tunnel. Ada Skerritt dies and the funeral is the cut's last great turnout. Boats sold cheap, sunk for insurance, broken up for iron. Lil runs *Patience* with a motor she cannot afford.
- **Nationalisation.** The cut belongs to the state and the state belongs to men like the Officer, now sitting for a Midlands seat. Tench is at the Ministry. The report that decides which canals live is being written by the people who own the lorries.
- **The decline.** The closures of the fifties, the last coal contracts, the first hire boats laughing at the working men. Askew gets older. Lil gets the boat. One chapter is the one where he dies, and the writers know which year and have not told anyone.

## The spine, stated once

The cut is being drained on purpose. Not by one villain but by a class of men who own the road and sit on the boards and write the reports, and who used the water while it was useful and are letting it silt now that it is not. Askew cannot stop it. Nobody could. The series is about a man who keeps his boat afloat in water that is being let out from under him, keeps moving, keeps his head down, takes what work he can find, does it well, and keeps his mouth shut about the one thing he could say that would sink the man at the top. Whether he ever says it is the series' last question.
