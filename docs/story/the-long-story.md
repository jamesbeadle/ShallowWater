# The long story

The spine of the series is a question whose answer keeps moving: **who runs this country, and what would it take to run it better?** In 1938 the answer is a class of men who own the road, sit on the boards and write the reports. They are letting the cut silt and the country with it. By 1958 the answer is Arthur Granger. The story is how he gets from one answer to the other, and what he does on the way.

The chapters after *The Tidal Thames* are written here as beats, not as missions. They show where each chapter has to start and end, and what it turns on. The twists are marked so they can be kept from anyone who should not read them.

## Locked (demo, autumn 1938)

A cadet runs from Whittington, and he has a reason. Granger is paid to bring him back, and what he finds is the reason. The depot armoury is short: someone has been signing for rifles that are not there. The rifles are leaving under coal on company boats that only run at night. The strike that shut the junctions was never a wage dispute. It was a curtain. The company let the keepers walk out and dropped the planks, and seven miles of water became a private yard. Crates could move from boat to lorry at Peel's Wharf with no police on the water and no boats passing. The strike fund is Dunmore's money, passed on by a union man who did not ask where it came from.

The demo also breaks his one rule. Three clients from three sides each want a man dead inside the seven miles he keeps clean. It ends with Granger knowing all of this and holding one crate. He chooses whether to return the cadet, run him, or bury the cadet's story with him. Then either the strike breaks or he breaks the junction. Either way the water opens.

*What he learns:* the keepers, the boat families and a runaway cadet did more for each other in six weeks than the company did for them in twenty years. Nobody was in charge of them, and he notices.

*One true thing about the war:* Garner, drunk, says Granger was the best shot the depot ever had, and that it was not for the shooting that they gave him the medal.

## The Grand Union (Game 2, 1938 to 1939)

The junctions open, and Granger follows the crates. From Fazeley he goes up the Curdworth locks into the city's black water and the Birmingham Canal Navigations, where a hundred miles of loops and basins can hide anything. The guns are going south on the Grand Union company's new motors, towards a ship for Spain. Dunmore is the road end. The water end is a Grand Union agent at Braunston who thinks he is a patriot. Tench has moved to a bigger toll house and kept the second set of books.

The player runs the Grand Union from Birmingham to the edge of London. Every stretch has its own crime and its own client. It ends at Paddington with the guns gone down onto the tideway, and with a name Granger did not want: the Officer's, on a letter Tench should not have kept.

*What he learns:* a ledger is a weapon. Tench never fired a shot, and every man on the Grand Union is afraid of him. Granger starts keeping books of his own: who paid him, for whom, and what they would give for it never to come out.

*One true thing:* the pistol in the locker belonged to a German officer, and Granger did not take it from a German.

## The Tidal Thames (Game 3, 1939)

*Sparrow* was never built for a tidal river. The tide runs faster than her engine, a tug's wash can sink her, and there is nowhere on the river to moor out of sight. The ship is here, and so is everyone who wants it stopped or sailed. Granger works the docks, Limehouse and the estuary for every side at once.

On the night tide he takes *Sparrow* past Westminster for the first time. The lights are on in the House. He ties up under the embankment wall for an hour, because it is the only slack water for a mile, and watches the members' cars come and go. Nobody looks down at the boat.

War is declared on a Sunday in September, halfway through the chapter. It ends with the ship sailed or sunk, and with Granger knowing that the Officer is a Member of Parliament now and was in that building the night *Sparrow* tied up beneath it.

*Twist:* the client who hired him on the Thames, the most decent man he meets in the chapter, is working for the Officer. Granger has been tidying up the Officer's mess all along.

*One true thing:* Lil Skerritt finds a photograph on the boat. It shows Granger in 1918 with a man whose face has been scratched out.

## Shallow Water 4: the war on the water (1940 to 1945)

Blackout on the cut, women crewing the boats and the barracks full. Garner's cadets go to France and some come back. The canals carry the war's coal, and Granger carries what the war cannot put on paper. He becomes the man the Ministry calls when it needs something done without a name on it.

The Officer is a Member of Parliament, a colonel again for the duration and a minister's friend. In the sergeants' mess Granger has not entered in ten years, he is asked to do once more what he did in 1918, for the same reason. This is the chapter that tells what he did: in the last month of that war he was ordered to shoot a man on his own side, and he did it. This time he says yes. He does the job, and he keeps the proof of who ordered it.

*Shock:* Garner, the one man who has always looked away for him, finds out what he did and will not look away. Granger cannot let him tell. How Garner dies is the player's choice, but not whether he dies.

*What he builds:* a network the length of the cut. Boat families, keepers, demobbed men and deserters he hid. They owe him, they trust him and they talk to each other. By VE Day it is the only thing in Britain that runs on time.

## Shallow Water 5: the peace that was promised (1945 to 1950)

The war is won and the promises are not kept. Rationing gets worse, homes are not built, and the men who ran the war are given the peace to run. The cut is nationalised, and Tench sits at the Ministry writing the report that decides which canals live. The road wins every argument.

Granger's network stops waiting. It starts with a strike he organises on the water, the kind the company faked in 1938, only real. It grows into a movement of boatmen, dockers, miners and the women who crewed the boats in the war. They call themselves the Cut. Granger is its gun and, more and more, its voice. The papers call him an outlaw. Sergeant Vokes, now an inspector with a file on four counties, is the man sent to bring him in.

*Bad things:* a bombing the movement needs and the papers blame on the other side, a union man with a family who will not fall in line, and a lie told to Lil that she believes for years.

*Twist:* Tench comes over. The clerk who kept everyone's books gives Granger the Ministry's. He never says why, and the series never tells.

## Shallow Water 6: the rising (1950 to 1955)

The Cut goes national. Granger is hunted, and the people hide him: in boat cabins, in pit villages, in a back room in every town the canals run behind. He becomes the man of the people because the people have watched him do for them what nobody else would.

The Officer, now a senior minister, offers him a deal: come in, take a seat and stop. Granger answers with the 1918 truth. He makes it public, and he makes his own part in it public too, because a man who confesses cannot be blackmailed. The Officer falls. Granger stands up in front of the country as a murderer who told the truth, and the country takes his side.

*Shock:* Lil, now running her own boat and the Cut's best organiser, finds out about the lie he told her in chapter 5. She does not leave. She becomes the one person who can tell him no, and she does.

*Twist:* Vokes is the one who arrests him in the end, and Vokes is the one who makes sure he lives to stand trial.

## Shallow Water 7: Westminster (1955 to 1958)

The trial, the pardon the crowd forces, and the election. The last chapter is played in London, on the water and in the corridors. Granger now has to beat the people he once worked for in their own building, with their own rules, and without a rifle. Every enemy he made in six chapters comes back, and so does every friend.

It ends with *Sparrow* moored on the Thames beside Parliament. The outlaw is now the man the country chose. Over the epilogue the country he builds is shown on the wireless and from the water: the canals clear and working, the homes built, the hospitals open, the coal and the boats carrying it. It is almost a utopia, and it is what could have been.

The last shot is Granger in *Sparrow*'s cabin at Westminster. He is sixty-five, alone, and keeps the locker locked. The rifle that does not exist is still in it.

## The spine, stated once

The country is being drained on purpose. No single villain is doing it. It is done by a class of men who own the road, sit on the boards and write the reports. They used the country while it was useful and are letting it silt now that it is not. In 1938 nobody can stop them. Granger spends twenty years learning how, and he does it with every tool they taught him: the rifle, the ledger, the lie and the crowd. The series asks whether a better country built that way is a better country at all, and it leaves the player to answer.
